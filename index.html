Welcome!

I'm a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Cambridge, supervised by Sebastian Graves and Eric French. My research fields are labor economics, macroeconomics, and applied microeconomics. I study how labor markets adjust to economic change, and what those adjustments mean for workers, firms, and public policy.

Before my PhD, I worked at the IMF and the World Bank on macro-fiscal forecasting, debt sustainability analysis, and DSGE modeling. I hold an MPA in International Finance and Economic Policy from Columbia University and a BA in International Studies from American University.

Job Market Paper

Cyclical Mismatch for New Hires: Evidence from Matched Vacancy-Worker Data

with Zara Contractor

This paper studies the cyclicality of skill mismatch between worker qualifications and job requirements. We construct a novel matched vacancy–worker panel of 37 million U.S. hires from 2010 to 2023 by linking vacancy postings to worker profiles. This panel lets us observe the mismatch between each worker’s qualifications and the requirements of their specific job, extending prior studies that assign the same requirement to all jobs within an occupation. We measure mismatch in two dimensions, years of relevant work experience and education level. Using a shift-share design, we find that weaker labor demand raises experience underqualification and lowers education underqualification among new hires. This response reflects firms adjusting by hiring workers with different qualifications for a job rather than by changing posted requirements for that job. We also document that the experience response reverses across periods. Weaker labor demand causes experience underqualification to fall in the 2010s but rise in the early 2020s, when experienced workers left the labor force in large numbers. To understand these contrasting patterns, we build a calibrated search model in which workers differ in experience and jobs differ in their experience requirements. The model shows that a fall in productivity lowers underqualification by bringing experienced workers into job search, consistent with our findings for the 2010s. On the other hand, an exit of experienced workers from the labor force raises underqualification by leaving fewer experienced job seekers, consistent with our findings for the early 2020s.
