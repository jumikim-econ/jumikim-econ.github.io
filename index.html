Welcome!

I'm a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Cambridge, supervised by Sebastian Graves and Eric French. My research fields are labor economics, macroeconomics, and applied microeconomics. I study how labor markets adjust to economic change, and what those adjustments mean for workers, firms, and public policy.

Before my PhD, I worked at the IMF and the World Bank on macro-fiscal forecasting, debt sustainability analysis, and DSGE modeling. I hold an MPA in International Finance and Economic Policy from Columbia University and a BA in International Studies from American University.

Job Market Paper

Cyclical Mismatch for New Hires: Evidence from Matched Vacancy-Worker Data

with Zara Contractor (Middlebury College)

We study the cyclicality of skill mismatch: how well worker qualifications match job requirements as economic conditions strengthen and weaken. We link worker profiles to vacancy postings to create a novel matched vacancy–worker panel of 37 million U.S. hires from 2010 to 2023. This panel lets us observe the mismatch between each worker’s qualifications and the requirements of their specific job, whereas prior studies of cyclical mismatch assign the same requirement to all jobs within an occupation. Yet, in our data, nearly two-thirds of the variation in posted requirements lies across jobs within the same occupation. We measure mismatch in two dimensions: years of relevant work experience and education level. Using a shift-share design, we find that experience underqualification is higher while education underqualification is lower in areas with weaker labor demand. Firms adjust mainly by hiring workers with different qualifications rather than by changing posted requirements. We also find that the experience response reverses across periods. Areas with weaker labor demand have lower experience underqualification in the 2010s but higher in the early 2020s, when experienced workers left the labor force in large numbers. To understand these contrasting patterns, we build a search model in which workers differ in experience and jobs differ in their experience requirements. Calibrated to our empirical results, the model shows that a fall in productivity brings experienced workers into job search, lowering underqualification, consistent with our findings for the 2010s. An exit of experienced workers from the labor force raises underqualification by leaving fewer experienced job seekers, consistent with our findings for the early 2020s.
